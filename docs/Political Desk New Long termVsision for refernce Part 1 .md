PHASE 0 — DESIGN PHILOSOPHY
Building the "GearCity of Politics"
0.1 The Mission

Create the most realistic, systemic, and emergent political simulation ever made.

Not an interactive novel.

Not a strategy game with politics.

Not a spreadsheet.

Not a sandbox full of disconnected mechanics.

Instead:

A living political ecosystem where every event emerges from thousands of interconnected simulations.

Politics is simply one layer of that ecosystem.

0.2 Core Philosophy

Everything in the game follows one rule:

Nothing important happens without a cause.

If inflation rises...

There must be reasons.

If an election is lost...

There must be reasons.

If a coup happens...

There must be reasons.

If a company collapses...

There must be reasons.

If crime rises...

There must be reasons.

The game should never rely on random modifiers like:

+10 Crime

Instead:

Police budget decreases
→ fewer officers
→ lower investigation rates
→ criminals become bolder
→ crime increases
→ businesses leave
→ unemployment rises
→ tax revenue falls
→ deficit grows

Everything is connected.

0.16 Complexity vs. Accessibility

0.17 The Three-Layer Model

Every mechanic should exist on three layers:

Simulation Layer

The hidden systems running continuously (citizens, economy, institutions, AI).

Decision Layer

The choices available to the player (laws, appointments, budgets, diplomacy).

Presentation Layer

How the player experiences the consequences (news, graphs, debates, advisor reports, elections, protests).

This separation lets the simulation stay deep while keeping the interface understandable.

Phase 0 Deliverable

At the end of Phase 0, we have the "constitution" for the entire project. Every future mechanic must satisfy these principles before it is added.

One addition I'd make beyond existing political sims

If the goal is truly GearCity-level depth, I'd add one guiding principle that none of the four benchmark games fully achieve:

Every institution should be playable, not just observable.

For example, if you're President, you shouldn't simply see "Education: 72%." You should be able to drill down into the Ministry of Education, inspect staffing, budgets, regional performance, reform proposals, and implementation bottlenecks. The same applies to ministries, courts, intelligence agencies, election commissions, and central banks. GearCity lets you inspect and influence the machinery; your political simulator should do the same for government.

That principle, more than adding hundreds of extra features, is what can make the game feel like the true "GearCity of politics."

Architectural recommendation before Phase 3

There's one major design decision I'd make now that will affect every later phase:

Do not make "country" the primary multiplayer unit. Make "society" the primary unit.

That means players should be able to participate in many different ways—politician, entrepreneur, journalist, judge, union leader, activist, military officer, academic, or ordinary citizen. Political power then becomes something players compete for within society rather than something granted simply because they selected a country. This creates a far richer multiplayer ecosystem and prevents countries from feeling empty unless someone is head of state.

3. Simulation Engine

5. Platform Engine

Owns networking, multiplayer synchronization, saves, analytics, modding, tooling, and live operations.

Every feature you've designed so far fits naturally into one of these engines. That separation gives you a clean, scalable architecture capable of supporting a long-lived multiplayer simulation rather than an increasingly tangled collection of systems.

The Biggest Missing Piece (My Recommendation)

After everything we've designed, I think we're still missing one system, and I would honestly call it Phase 4.5 because it sits between political parties and government.

Political Capital Gameplay

This is inspired by Suzerain, The Political Process, and real politics.

Every major decision consumes political capital, not just money.

For example:

You win with 230 seats out of 400.

You have:

Political Capital: 100
Administrative Capacity: 72
Public Trust: 61
Information Credibility: 68

Passing a controversial labor reform might cost 25 political capital. Negotiating with coalition partners could recover some support or consume more of it. Choosing to spend your limited capital becomes a strategic decision, much like allocating limited resources in GearCity.

That turns governing into a game of priorities rather than simply enacting every desirable policy immediately.

One Thing I Would Change from Our Earlier Design

There's one assumption I'd revisit:

One player = one party forever.

I still think one player should control only one political party at a time, but I would not permanently bind the player to that party.

Instead:

You can resign as leader.
Your party continues under AI leadership.
After a cooldown or under defined rules, you can found a new party or join an existing one.
Your past actions and reputation follow you.

This better reflects real politics, where politicians sometimes leave parties, form new ones, or return after time away. It also creates richer long-term multiplayer stories without allowing players to control multiple parties simultaneously.

After thinking through the first hour, I no longer think "movement" should be the first organization.

      ↓

      ↓

Why add Campaign Organization?

Because it gives players a meaningful intermediate goal:

It's small and personal.
You recruit your first volunteers.
You build your public identity.
You organize local events.
You learn the game's systems.
It has very low stakes.

Only after proving you can organize people does it evolve into a larger political movement, and eventually—if the country's legal requirements are met—a registered political party.

I think this progression is more engaging, more realistic, and solves the "what do I do in my first few hours?" problem much better than jumping straight into founding a movement. It also gives you a clean, satisfying sense of growth at every stage of a long political career.
